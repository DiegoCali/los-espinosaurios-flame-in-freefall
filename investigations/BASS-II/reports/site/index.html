Measuring and forecasting flame spread in microgravity from ISS video

An automatic computer-vision pipeline tracks the flame front in the Burning and Suppression of Solids II videos, converts it to millimetres with the duct's own ruler, compares the spread rates with those NASA investigators published, and forecasts how the flame front evolves.

How the measurement works

  1. Download each test's side-view video from the NASA PSI repository (720×480, 29.97 fps, non-square pixels corrected).
  2. Read the burned-in telemetry (air velocity, fan voltage, radiometer, clock) by template OCR and mask it out.
  3. Skip frames with the cabin lights on, mask static artefacts, and segment the luminous flame with thresholds relative to each frame's noise so faint blue edges survive.
  4. Convert pixels to millimetres with the duct's ruler, visible in the illuminated frames before ignition.
  5. Split the front's trajectory into linear regimes (ignition, spread under flow, spread after a flow change, extinction) and fit the steady spread rate with a robust Theil-Sen regression.
  6. Backtest forecasting models on the measured series and compare the rates with published values.

One test, end to end: B1 (100 µm PMMA film, opposed flow)

How close is it to NASA's published numbers?

The spatial calibration uses no published value. Published rates of the development tests (B1–B5, B7, B12, M1) were used to choose the segmentation and regime settings, so their accuracy is in-sample. The hold-out slab tests were processed only after every setting was frozen. A physics review that flagged implausible results was done with the published values visible, so filtered statistics are shown next to unfiltered ones.

Development tests: median error 4.5 %, bias +2.1 %. Hold-out: 13 slab tests were processed once with frozen settings; 12 gave at least one comparison (M15 showed no steady spread). Across their 14 flow-step comparisons the median error is 18 %; these tests were not part of the physics review. Split by flow step: the first or only step of a test gives a median error of 15 % (n = 4, bias -1 %), while later steps after a flow change give 64 % (n = 10). A thick slab needs minutes to settle after the flow changes and the per-step window does not yet wait for that, so later-step rates stay close to the previous step's rate. For slab tests with several flow steps, 14 of 32 published steps could be matched to a measured flow plateau. Thick slabs burn for 10 to 30 minutes with very dim late phases, which is where the automatic regime choice is least reliable.

What the whole dataset shows

83 tests were processed. A physics review classified them as: 34 excluded, 10 flagged, 1 order of magnitude, 15 trusted, 9 trusted null, 1 trusted steps only, 13 unreviewed. Only the 15 trusted tests enter the ensemble figures below; excluded tests are missed dim burns, igniter-flash or ignition-growth regimes, rods where the video measures flame-length settling rather than spread, or tests with too little footage.

Forecasting the flame front

Each steady-spread series of the trusted tests is cut at several points in time and every model forecasts the next 1 to 10 seconds of the leading-edge position. MASE below 1 beats a naive one-step forecast; coverage is the share of observations inside the 90 % interval. The steady windows are chosen with hindsight, so these numbers describe forecasting within a steady regime.

Limitations

  • The ruler graduation (1 cm major marks) is inferred from the 76 mm duct geometry; NASA documents do not state it.
  • Development-test accuracy is in-sample; the hold-out slab steps are the out-of-sample evidence.
  • For thick slabs, rates after a flow change are over-estimated because the measurement window starts before the flame re-equilibrates.
  • Faint flames depend on the segmentation threshold (figure G15); that systematic uncertainty is not in the per-test intervals.
  • Segmentation is scored on synthetic flames until a hand-labelled gold set exists.
  • The B14, B18 and B20 main videos are truncated on the NASA server; their original MPEG-TS recordings were used instead.
